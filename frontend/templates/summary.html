What this research asked

Before a model is trained, a series of decisions is made about the data: how to turn categories into numbers, what to do about missing values, whether to put features on a common scale, whether to drop some features, and whether to search for better model settings. These decisions are usually made by habit. This project measured whether they actually change the result, and by how much.

The short answer: most of them do not, and the ones that do depend on which model you are using rather than being general rules.

How this is measured

For every combination of dataset and model, the best and worst scoring levels of a factor are compared, and the gap is divided by the baseline score so that datasets with different target scales can be averaged together. The table shows the median over 18 datasets. It measures how much a choice can move the score, not whether any particular level is better. Injected-missingness sweeps are excluded, because there the missing-data rate is something the experiment set rather than a property of the dataset.

Which factors produced a real, tested effect

Of 167 statistical comparisons, 25 survived correction for multiple testing. Most preprocessing choices produced nothing detectable. These two counts come from the full statistical pass recorded in the findings report; the Findings page re-runs the same tests per sweep on demand, because running all of them at once would make this page slow to load.

Imputation dominates only because it was measured at six different missing-data settings, not because it is intrinsically the most important factor.

Scaling depends entirely on the model

Scale your features for SVM and linear models. Do not bother for random forests or gradient boosting.

Logistic regression is the only model with a statistically confirmed gain (better on 10 of 11 datasets). Tree ensembles move by hundredths of a percent.

A case where not scaling silently broke the model

On the house_16H dataset, one feature had a standard deviation near 66,000 while the rest were below 0.34. The solver inside scikit-learn discards directions whose scale falls below a threshold relative to the largest, so it fitted the unscaled data using only 9 of the 16 features and reported no error. Scaling restored all 16 and lowered the error by 7.7 percent. Loosening the solver's tolerance on the raw data reproduced the scaled result exactly, confirming the cause. On data with one dominant-scale feature, failing to scale can silently remove features from a linear model.

Feature selection is usually harmless and occasionally destroys the model

Prefer recursive elimination over single-feature filters. The average case hides the risk.

The typical effect is under 4 percent. On energy-efficiency, two filter methods raised gradient boosting's error more than sixfold, from 0.47 to 3.08, by discarding features the model needed together.

Tuning cost two thirds of the compute for one result

Budget hyperparameter tuning for gradient boosting. Treat it as optional elsewhere.

Grid, random and Bayesian search were indistinguishable from each other. Note the right panel is in percent error reduced while the other two are in F1 points, so bar heights are not comparable between panels.

Two results the system caught and withdrew

Both would have been reported as findings by a less instrumented study. They are included because a method that cannot catch its own errors cannot be trusted when it reports a success.

A significant result that was an artefact

An earlier sweep found Bayesian search improving the SVM. Every hyperparameter grid held 4 to 6 candidates while the random search drew 10, so the search exhausted the grid and silently became grid search on all 56 model and dataset pairs. Given a genuine continuous range to search, the same comparison is no longer significant and the gain reverses on two datasets.

A comparison that was measuring the wrong thing

Deleting rows with missing values appeared to beat imputing them on regression. It was being scored on different data: on one dataset it kept 38 of 252 rows and was measured on the easy remainder. Withdrawn as a measurement artefact.

Results do not depend on how they were measured

Repeating the measurement with a completely different splitting scheme reproduced the same answers, so the findings are not an artefact of the evaluation method.

A limit that was discovered, not designed around

The feature-selection results included five cases where the overall test was clearly significant but no individual comparison survived. This was not a weak effect. With 7 datasets and 4 options to compare, the smallest result the test can possibly produce is already above the threshold for significance, so nothing could have been confirmed regardless of how large the real effect was.

The lesson: check what the smallest achievable result is before collecting data, not after.

What to do with this

  • Scale features for SVM and linear models; skip it for tree ensembles.
  • Treat scaling as a correctness issue when feature scales differ by orders of magnitude, not just a performance tweak.
  • Encoding choice only matters in proportion to how much of your data is categorical. Avoid ordinal encoding for linear models.
  • Impute rather than delete rows, and be suspicious of any comparison where the baseline is scored on fewer rows.
  • Use kNN imputation when a substantial share of data is missing; the choice does not measurably matter when little is missing.
  • Spend tuning budget on gradient boosting. No search strategy beat any other, so the cheapest one is the right one.

Full detail is in the research report, the findings report and the meta-analysis in the project's documentation folder.
